How to Fire Your HOA Management Company

A step-by-step guide to terminating your management contract, documenting failures, securing association records, and making a clean transition to a new company or self-management.

Most HOA boards don't fire their management company because they're afraid of the transition. But keeping a bad management company costs the association far more - in missed deadlines, sloppy financials, ignored maintenance, and frustrated homeowners. This guide walks you through the entire process, from documenting failures to making a clean transition.

Signs Your Management Company Needs to Go

If several of these apply, the relationship is probably beyond repair:

  • Financial reports are consistently late, incomplete, or contain errors
  • Board requests go unanswered for weeks
  • Vendor contracts are not competitively bid
  • Maintenance requests from homeowners are ignored or delayed
  • The manager doesn't attend board meetings or comes unprepared
  • Delinquency collections are not being pursued
  • Insurance policies lapse or are inadequate
  • The management company has RICO complaints or regulatory issues
  • Communication with homeowners is poor or nonexistent
  • You're paying full-service fees but getting minimal service

Step 1 - Review Your Management Contract

Before you do anything else, pull out the management agreement and read it end to end. You need to know exactly what you agreed to and how to get out of it.

  • Contract term and auto-renewal clause: Many contracts auto-renew for additional one-year terms unless you give written notice within a specific window (often 60-90 days before expiration). Miss that window and you may be locked in for another year.
  • Termination clause: Most contracts require 30-90 days written notice of termination. Find the exact requirement.
  • Early termination penalties: Some contracts include a fee for terminating before the end of the term. Know the dollar amount before you vote.
  • "Cause" vs. "without cause" termination: Termination "for cause" (documented failures, breach of contract) may waive early termination penalties and allow shorter notice periods. Termination "without cause" (you just want to switch) typically requires full notice and may trigger penalties.
  • Included vs. separately billed services: Identify what's in the base fee and what they charge extra for. This matters when comparing proposals from replacement companies.
  • Association records: Your association's records belong to the association, not the management company. The contract should confirm this, but even if it doesn't, this is generally the law.

Step 2 - Document Everything

Before you bring a termination vote to the board, build your case. This documentation protects the board if the company disputes the termination or claims you breached the contract.

  • Create a timeline of failures with specific dates
  • Keep copies of unanswered emails and requests
  • Note every missed deadline - financial reports, meeting notices, maintenance follow-ups
  • Record each contractual obligation that was not met
  • If homeowners have complained, collect those complaints in writing

Use a simple tracking table:

Date Issue Contract Section Status
01/15/2026 December financial report not delivered Section 4.C Unresolved
02/03/2026 Board email re: vendor contract unanswered Section 3.F No response after 3 follow-ups
03/10/2026 Manager absent from board meeting Section 3.G No notice or substitute provided

Step 3 - Know Your Contractual Duties (What They Owe You)

A typical management contract obligates the company to perform these core duties. Compare this list against your actual contract - every item they're not doing is a documented failure:

  1. Maintenance of common elements and property - routine inspections, vendor scheduling, work order follow-up
  2. Collection of assessments, fees, and charges - billing, payment processing, delinquency notices
  3. Preparation and delivery of monthly financial statements - typically due by the 25th of the following month
  4. Budget preparation - typically required 135 days before the fiscal year end
  5. Payroll, tax filings, and employee management - if the association has direct employees
  6. Contract negotiation with vendors and service providers - competitive bidding, contract review, performance oversight
  7. Attendance at all board meetings with prepared reports - financial summaries, maintenance updates, delinquency reports

Go through your contract section by section. Mark every duty they're failing to perform. This becomes the factual basis for a "for cause" termination if your contract distinguishes between cause and without-cause termination.

Step 4 - Get a Board Vote

Terminating the management company requires a formal motion at a properly noticed board meeting. This is a board decision - it does not typically require a homeowner vote, but check your governing documents to be sure. Some CC&Rs or bylaws require owner approval for management changes.

Sample Motion

"I move that the Board terminate the management agreement with [Company Name] effective [Date], pursuant to Section [X] of the management contract, and authorize the President to deliver written notice of termination."

  • Record the vote in the official minutes
  • The vote does not need to be unanimous, but should have a clear majority
  • If a board member has a conflict of interest (e.g., personal relationship with the management company), they should recuse themselves from the vote
  • Keep the discussion factual - reference the documented failures from Step 2

Step 5 - Send Written Notice

After the board votes, send the termination notice in writing. This letter may end up in court, so keep it professional and factual.

  • Send via certified mail, return receipt requested - this proves delivery
  • Reference the specific contract section authorizing termination
  • State the effective date of termination
  • Request return of all association records, financial documents, bank account access, vendor contracts, keys, access codes, and passwords
  • Set a deadline for records transfer - typically 30 days
  • Keep the tone professional - no accusations, no emotional language, just facts and dates

Use the demand letter template as a starting framework and adapt it for the termination notice.

Step 6 - Plan the Transition

This is where most boards stumble. Start planning the transition before you send the termination notice. Here is a checklist of everything you need to secure from the outgoing management company:

Transition Checklist

  • Bank accounts - change signers immediately upon the effective date
  • All financial records, general ledgers, and tax filings
  • Insurance policies and certificates of insurance
  • Vendor contracts (landscaping, janitorial, security, elevator, pool, etc.)
  • Employee records, if the association has direct employees
  • Homeowner contact database and unit records
  • Access codes, keys, gate remotes, and mailbox keys
  • Website and homeowner portal access credentials
  • Pending legal matters and attorney correspondence
  • Delinquency records and collection files
  • Architectural review applications in progress
  • Open maintenance requests and work orders

Step 7 - Find a Replacement (or Self-Manage)

Start this process while you're still in the notice period so there's no gap in management coverage.

Hiring a New Management Company

  • Get proposals from at least three management companies
  • Ask for references from associations of similar size and type
  • Key questions to ask every candidate:
    • How many properties does each manager handle? (Lower is better - look for 6-8 max)
    • What are your response time guarantees for board and homeowner inquiries?
    • What financial reporting software do you use, and can the board access it directly?
    • Do you provide a board portal with real-time access to financials and work orders?
    • What is your policy on competitive bidding for vendor contracts?
  • Overlap the new company with the outgoing company for 2-4 weeks if possible - this makes the records transfer much smoother

Self-Management

If your association is small (under 50 units) and has engaged, competent board members, self-management can save significant money. But it requires real commitment - someone has to handle financials, vendor management, homeowner communications, and compliance. Consider a hybrid model where you self-manage but hire a bookkeeper for financial reporting and a part-time administrator for day-to-day tasks.

What to Do If They Push Back

Some management companies do not go quietly. Here's how to handle common pushback tactics:

  • They claim you owe early termination fees: Review the contract carefully. If you're terminating for cause (documented failures), the early termination penalty may not apply. Have your attorney review this.
  • They delay returning records: Your state law almost certainly requires prompt return of association records. Send a written demand with a specific deadline citing the applicable statute.
  • They hold records hostage over disputed fees: This is typically illegal. Association records belong to the association regardless of any fee dispute. Document the refusal and consult your attorney.
  • They claim the termination wasn't valid: This is why documentation matters. You have the board minutes showing the vote, the certified mail receipt showing delivery, and the contract section authorizing termination.
  • File a complaint: If they refuse to return records or cooperate with the transition, file a complaint with your state's real estate commission or HOA regulatory body.

The First 90 Days After the Switch

The transition doesn't end when the new company starts. Use the first 90 days to audit and reset:

  • Audit all financial records from the previous management company - look for discrepancies, missing payments, or improperly categorized expenses
  • Verify all vendor contracts are current and competitively priced - the old manager may have been using preferred vendors at above-market rates
  • Send a welcome letter to all homeowners introducing the new management company with updated contact information
  • Review insurance coverage to ensure all policies are current and adequate
  • Set up new communication channels - email list, homeowner portal, newsletter, or whatever works for your community
  • Schedule a 90-day review with the new management company to evaluate performance against the contract terms - don't let problems accumulate this time
