HOA Board Meeting Minutes Template

Proper meeting minutes are the legal record of every decision your HOA board makes. Use this template to ensure minutes are complete, accurate, and compliant with your state's requirements.

Why Proper Meeting Minutes Matter

Board meeting minutes are not just a formality. They are the official legal record of every action your HOA board takes, and in most states, they are required by statute. Minutes serve three critical purposes:

  • Legal record of decisions. Minutes document every motion, vote, and resolution. If a dispute arises over what the board authorized, the minutes are the definitive source. Courts treat properly recorded minutes as evidence of board action.
  • Transparency for homeowners. Most state HOA statutes require that meeting minutes be made available to members upon request. Incomplete or missing minutes make it impossible for homeowners to hold the board accountable.
  • State law compliance. Nearly every state with HOA legislation requires boards to keep minutes of all meetings. Failure to maintain minutes can expose the board to legal liability, invalidate decisions, and create grounds for a member challenge.

Whether you are a board secretary drafting minutes or a homeowner reviewing them, this template covers the standard format recognized by parliamentary procedure and most state statutes.

Note: Check your state statutes and governing documents for any specific requirements about what must be included in meeting minutes. Some states require that minutes include the exact text of every motion, while others require only that actions taken be recorded.

Motion Tracking Format

Use this standalone format to track individual motions across meetings. Keeping a separate motion log makes it easy to follow up on implementation and verify that approved actions were carried out.

Tips for Accurate Meeting Minutes

What Minutes Should (and Should Not) Include

Minutes should record actions taken, not conversations had. Include: the type of meeting, date, time, location, who was present, whether a quorum existed, every motion (exact text), who moved and seconded, the vote result, and the time of adjournment. Do not include verbatim dialogue, personal opinions of the secretary, or editorial commentary on the quality of discussion. If a director requests that their dissenting vote be recorded by name, include it.

Recording Motions Correctly

Every motion must include five elements: (1) the exact text of what was proposed, (2) who moved it, (3) who seconded it, (4) whether discussion occurred, and (5) the result of the vote. If a motion is amended, record both the original motion and the amended version that was ultimately voted on. A motion that dies for lack of a second should still be recorded, noting "No second; motion failed."

When Minutes Become Legal Documents

Minutes become the official record once approved by the board at the next meeting. Until approved, they are "draft minutes." Most states require that approved minutes be available to members within a set number of days. In legal proceedings, courts give significant weight to approved minutes as evidence of what the board authorized. If the minutes say it happened, courts will generally presume it did. If the minutes don't mention it, courts may presume it didn't.

Robert's Rules Quick Reference for Minutes

Under Robert's Rules of Order, the secretary is responsible for minutes. Key rules: minutes should be written in the third person ("The board voted..." not "We voted..."), minutes should record what was done rather than what was said, and the secretary should never include their own opinion. The approval of minutes at the following meeting is the opportunity for any director to request corrections. Once approved, minutes should not be altered except by a formal motion to amend.

How to Handle Executive Session Minutes

When the board enters executive (closed) session, a separate set of minutes should be kept. Executive session minutes typically record only that the session occurred, who was present, the general topic (such as "personnel matter" or "pending litigation"), and any motions made. Executive session minutes are generally not available to the general membership but must still be maintained as part of the association's official records. Check your state statute for specific requirements, as some states limit what topics may be discussed in executive session.
