Property Rights Guide

Eminent Domain and HOA Property Rights

When the government wants your community's land, here is what the law says, how the process works, and what your HOA board and homeowners can do to protect their interests.

Disclaimer: This guide is educational and based on general legal principles and real HOA experience dealing with a government land request. Laws vary by state and jurisdiction. Consult a licensed attorney for advice about your specific situation.

What Is Eminent Domain

Eminent domain is the government's power to take private property for public use. It is authorized by the Fifth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution, which states: "nor shall private property be taken or damaged for public use without just compensation."

Every state constitution has a similar provision. In Hawaii, Article I, Section 20 of the State Constitution reads: "Private property shall not be taken or damaged for public use without just compensation."

The key phrase is "just compensation." The government can take your property, but it must pay you fair market value for it.

How the Process Works

Eminent domain does not happen overnight. It follows a defined legal process with multiple stages where property owners and HOAs have the right to respond:

Stage 1: Initial Contact

A government agency (city, county, state, or federal) or a public utility approaches the property owner or HOA with a proposition. This can happen at a public hearing, through a letter, or through direct contact. At this stage, the government is expressing interest -- nothing has been decided.

Stage 2: Inspection and Appraisal

The government requests access to the property to perform an environmental assessment, survey, or appraisal. Granting access for an inspection does not imply consent to proceed with construction or acquisition. This is a critical point. Your HOA board should explicitly state this in writing when responding to any access request.

Stage 3: Offer of Just Compensation

After the appraisal, the government makes a formal offer. This offer must represent what the government considers to be the fair market value of the property or the portion being taken. You are not required to accept this offer.

Stage 4: Negotiation or Condemnation

If the offer is rejected, the government can either negotiate a higher price or file a condemnation action in court. In court, a judge or jury determines the fair market value. The property owner has the right to present their own appraisal and evidence of value.

From experience: When a government agency approached our HOA about accessing community land, the board agreed to allow a letter requesting access for an environmental assessment -- with explicit written language stating "no implied consent to proceed with construction." This preserved all of the association's rights while allowing the required assessment to take place.

"Just Compensation" -- What It Actually Means

Just compensation is defined as the fair market value of the property at the time of the taking. But fair market value is not always straightforward, especially for HOA common areas:

  • Common areas: How do you value a community pool, park, or access road? The appraisal must consider what these areas contribute to the overall property values of individual units.
  • Partial takings: If only part of the property is taken, the compensation must include both the value of the land taken and any reduction in value to the remaining property (called "severance damages").
  • Inverse condemnation: If the government damages your property without formally taking it (flooding, construction vibration, loss of access), you may have a claim for inverse condemnation. The landmark case Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co. established that a physical invasion caused by government action constitutes a taking even without formal condemnation.

What Your HOA Board Should Do

If your HOA receives a request from a government agency regarding community property:

  1. Do not agree to anything in the initial meeting. Thank the representative, take their information, and tell them the board will respond after review.
  2. Hire an attorney immediately. This is not a situation for DIY legal work. You need an attorney who specializes in eminent domain or real property law. The cost of an attorney is far less than the cost of accepting an inadequate offer.
  3. Get your own appraisal. Do not rely solely on the government's appraisal. An independent appraisal gives you negotiating leverage.
  4. Research the case law. Understanding how courts in your state have ruled on similar takings helps you and your attorney build a stronger position. In Hawaii, Hawaii Housing Authority v. Lyman (68 H. 55, 1985) is a foundational case.
  5. Communicate with homeowners. The membership has a right to know what is happening. Hold a special meeting, present the facts, and get member input before making any decisions.
  6. Document everything. Every conversation, letter, email, and meeting note related to the government's request should be preserved. This becomes evidence if the case goes to court.
  7. Guard access carefully. If the government requests access for inspection, provide it in writing with clear conditions: the scope of the inspection, the timeframe, and an explicit statement that access does not constitute consent to acquisition or construction.

When the Government Oversteps

Not every government action that affects property value constitutes a taking. But some actions cross the line:

  • Physical occupation: If the government physically occupies your property (even temporarily), it is a taking requiring compensation.
  • Regulatory taking: If a regulation eliminates all economically beneficial use of the property, it may be a taking even without physical occupation.
  • Excessive conditions: If the government conditions a permit on giving up property rights, and the condition is not proportional to the project's impact, it may be an unconstitutional taking.

Your Rights at Every Stage

  • You have the right to refuse the initial offer
  • You have the right to hire your own appraiser
  • You have the right to negotiate for a higher price
  • You have the right to challenge the taking in court
  • You have the right to recover attorney's fees if the court awards you more than the government offered
  • You have the right to be present and heard at any public hearing
  • You have the right to receive relocation assistance if you are displaced (under the Uniform Relocation Act)

Key Legal References

  • U.S. Constitution, Fifth Amendment -- "nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation"
  • Hawaii Constitution, Article I, Section 20 -- "Private property shall not be taken or damaged for public use without just compensation"
  • Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act -- Federal protections for displaced property owners
  • Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co. (1872) -- Physical invasion by government action constitutes a taking
  • Hawaii Housing Authority v. Lyman (68 H. 55, 1985) -- Hawaii eminent domain case law
  • Kelo v. City of New London (2005) -- Expanded definition of "public use" to include economic development (controversial; many states have since enacted stricter limits)
